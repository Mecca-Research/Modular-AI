This premise has teeth. 🪨⛏️ The mine is not merely a resource node, it is the **engine that slowly turns a wrecked slave colony into a functioning dwarf town**. At first Lord Dwarf owns a hole in a mountain, broken carts, several miserable buildings, and a workforce whose collective management philosophy is roughly *“sit on crate until somebody explains what the hell is happening.”* 😆

# Resource progression

I'd divide realistic resources into economic eras.

### Tier 0: The mountain itself

Stone, clay, sand, timber from nearby forests, limestone.

These barely make money.

But they allow the colony to **rebuild itself cheaply**.

A dwarf spends three days cutting stone and produces enough material to repair four dormitory walls. That does not look impressive on the balance sheet, but it raises:

**Housing Quality +8**
**Morale +3**
**Disease Resistance +2**
**Rest Efficiency +5%**

Now economics and civilization-building interlock.

### Tier 1: Survival economy

Coal
Iron
Copper
Salt
Clay
Limestone

These create your first positive industrial loop.

> Mine iron → make tools → better tools → mine more iron.

And:

> Mine coal → hotter furnaces → better iron → stronger supports → deeper tunnels.

That recursive progression should be one of the game's central pleasures.

### Tier 2: Skilled metallurgy

Tin
Zinc
Lead
Nickel
Manganese
Graphite
Sulfur

Now specialized professions appear.

A generic **Miner** eventually branches into:

Miner → Veteran Miner → Vein Specialist
Miner → Blaster
Miner → Shaft Engineer
Miner → Surveyor
Miner → Foreman

Meanwhile metallurgy evolves:

Smelter → Metallurgist → Alloy Master.

Bronze, brass, high-quality steels, specialized tools and commercial manufactured products suddenly become possible.

### Tier 3: Wealth extraction

Silver
Gold
Gemstones
Fine marble
High-grade crystal

This is where Lord Dwarf finally sees serious money.

But there should be a wonderful economic trap here:

A crude workforce finding gold is actually less profitable than a highly developed workforce mining ordinary materials.

Why?

Because inexperienced miners destroy deposits.

Imagine:

**Raw emerald cluster discovered.**

Untrained dwarf with pickaxe:

> “GREEN ROCK!”

WHACK.

Estimated intact value: 2,400 crowns

Now you need a **Gem Extraction Specialist**.

### Tier 4: Strategic minerals

Tungsten
Chromium
Cobalt
Molybdenum
Platinum
Rare specialty ores

These don't merely sell.

They unlock technological superiority.

Your mine slowly transitions from:

**resource extraction company**

to

**vertically integrated dwarf industrial empire.**

You stop selling iron ore.

You sell tools.

Then machinery.

Then pumps.

Then mine carts.

Then precision equipment.

Eventually other kingdoms are buying **DwarfWorks™ industrial systems** manufactured from your mountain.

That is where the business-management game can become enormous.

Every discovered material should have four separate values:

**Knowledge → Extraction → Processing → Market**

**Unknown metallic vein**

> “Probably iron.”

> “Copper-rich deposit containing 0.7% cobalt and unusually high silver concentrations.”

> “There's a narrow gold-bearing quartz structure behind it. Do not blast this wall.”

# The First Management System: Capability

Every dwarf has several fundamental characteristics.

Not hundreds of RPG attributes.

Something legible.

| Attribute | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Capability** | How much useful work the dwarf can perform |
| **Skill** | How effectively they perform a specific type of work |
| **Energy** | How long they can work before exhaustion |
| **Motivation** | Willingness to actually use capability |
| **Experience** | Accumulated practical knowledge |
| **Condition** | Injury, illness, hunger, fatigue |
| **Discipline** | Ability to follow organized work routines |
| **Initiative** | Ability to operate without direct instruction |

This creates a very important distinction.

A dwarf can possess:

**Capability: 8**

but:

**Motivation: 23%**

Meaning you're effectively getting:

**1.84 units of useful labor.**

That dwarf isn't necessarily inherently lazy.

Maybe he sleeps on wet straw, eats boiled turnips, owns a cracked pickaxe, hasn't been paid properly in months, and has watched four previous owners disappear.

His motivation makes perfect sense. 😂

# The Fundamental Production Equation

Something like:

$$
W_i = C_i \times S_i \times E_i \times M_i \times Q_i
$$

Where:

$C$ = capability
$S$ = skill modifier
$E$ = available energy
$M$ = motivation
$Q$ = equipment/workplace quality

The really important thing is that **none of these permanently substitute for the others**.

You can throw twenty untrained dwarves at a limestone face.

That works.

You cannot throw twenty untrained dwarves at:

an unstable deep shaft
precision gem extraction
ventilation design
blast planning
high-pressure pumping
complex smelting

Eventually the brute-force economy hits a wall.

That is your transition from:

## Manpower Civilization

to

## Engineering Civilization

And that should feel enormous.

Even though Lord Dwarf can move around continuously, economically the game behaves almost like a turn-based management game hidden inside a dungeon crawler.

At dawn, you have a finite pool of available dwarf labor. Each dwarf begins the day with a certain amount of usable work energy based on rest, health, food, morale, equipment, and skill.

You then allocate them across the estate.

Some mine.

Some cut timber.

Some repair tools.

Some construct buildings.

Some process ore.

Some study.

Some teach.

Some work in town businesses.

Some may not be available at all because they are traveling, injured, raising children, training, or resting.

Once the useful workforce is exhausted, the productive day is effectively over.

Then comes **Lord Dwarf's night shift**.

Inventory arrives.

Workers are paid.

Tools are inspected.

Market prices update.

Construction progresses.

Reports come in.

Workers socialize.

Relationships develop.

Businesses make sales.

Your dwarves recover.

You decide what tomorrow's workforce will do.

That creates a very satisfying rhythm:

\[
\text{Plan}
\rightarrow
\text{Deploy}
\rightarrow
\text{Produce}
\rightarrow
\text{Return}
\rightarrow
\text{Account}
\rightarrow
\text{Invest}
\rightarrow
\text{Plan Again}
\]

The mountain is the daytime adventure. The ledger is the nighttime adventure. 📒

## Tools become multiplicative, not cosmetic

Your wear/durability/craftsmanship relationship is exactly right.

Every tool can have four main properties:

\[
T = \{Q,C,D,W\}
\]

Where:

\(Q\) = current quality/performance  
\(C\) = craftsmanship  
\(D\) = maximum durability  
\(W\) = current wear

Craftsmanship determines both how effective the tool is and how slowly it deteriorates.

So a crude pick might have:

**Craftsmanship:** 31  
**Durability:** 44  
**Wear:** 63%  
**Extraction Bonus:** +8%

While the one surviving excellent pickaxe might be:

**Craftsmanship:** 92  
**Durability:** 91  
**Wear:** 8%  
**Extraction Bonus:** +34%

That perfect pickaxe is therefore not merely "better loot."

It's a **reference artifact**.

Your smith can examine it.

Your apprentices can try to reproduce it.

Eventually:

> "Make every pickaxe at least this good."

That gives craftsmanship an actual technological history.

## Worker energy versus task energy

This should be the fundamental production calculation.

Every task requires a certain quantity of effective work:

\[
E_{\text{required}}
\]

Each dwarf contributes:

\[
E_i^{\text{effective}}
=
E_i
\times
S_i
\times
M_i
\times
T_i
\times
K_i
\]

Where:

\(E_i\) = physical energy available  
\(S_i\) = task skill  
\(M_i\) = motivation  
\(T_i\) = tool efficiency  
\(K_i\) = knowledge/experience modifier

A novice dwarf might spend **20 raw energy** while only contributing **9 effective task energy**.

A veteran with a fine tool might spend the same 20 physical energy but contribute **31 effective work**.

That is how expertise becomes meaningful without turning dwarves into superheroes.

## Your "critical extraction" idea is especially good

Skill should not merely increase average speed.

Experienced workers occasionally perform **high-efficiency actions**.

A novice swings repeatedly at bad angles.

A veteran recognizes:

> strike here.

CRACK.

A huge usable section separates.

Mechanically that becomes something like a critical work event.

Examples:

**Clean Break**  
Extra ore extracted with no additional energy.

**Vein Recognition**  
Worker identifies a higher-grade section.

**Efficient Cut**  
Reduces tool wear.

**Material Preservation**  
Less ore becomes waste.

**Hazard Recognition**  
Stops work before support failure.

**Workflow Insight**  
Temporarily improves the entire crew.

This gives workers personality through competence.

## Task sites have labor capacity

This is essential because otherwise the optimal strategy becomes:

> dwarf avalanche.

Every worksite should therefore have:

**Base Work Slots**

and

**Maximum Effective Crew Size**.

Suppose a narrow ore face supports:

**Optimal crew: 3**

Assign:

1 dwarf → 46% potential output  
2 dwarves → 81%  
3 dwarves → 100%  
4 dwarves → 111%  
5 dwarves → 117%  
8 dwarves → 123%

At some point you're just placing dwarves behind other dwarves.

One is mining.

Seven are professionally observing his backside. 😂

So output could use a diminishing-return curve such as:

\[
P(n)=P_{\max}(1-e^{-kn})
\]

You don't need to show that formula to the player. They just see:

> **Crew congestion: Severe**

Then technology increases the effective work area.

Blasting creates larger faces.

Drills allow simultaneous work.

Rails remove ore faster.

Lighting increases useful space.

Mechanical crushers eliminate manual tasks.

Engineering does not simply provide "+15% production."

It **changes the geometry of labor**.

That's much more interesting.

## Waste becomes increasingly important

Early extraction might look like:

100 units raw rock

produces:

12 iron-bearing material  
3 copper-bearing material  
1 useful quartz  
84 waste rock

At first, waste rock is just dumped.

Later you realize:

some becomes construction aggregate.

Some becomes road material.

Advanced processing later recovers trace minerals.

So efficiency is also:

\[
\text{Useful Output}
=
\text{Primary Product}
+
\text{Byproducts}
+
\text{Recovered Waste}
\]

A developed dwarf economy eventually hates throwing anything away.

Very appropriate. 😆

## Forestry provides the first labor competition

This is where the game teaches opportunity cost.

You have twelve dwarves.

Perhaps:

5 mine  
3 chop wood  
2 repair infrastructure  
1 sorts ore  
1 cooks

Immediately the player thinks:

> If I move the woodcutters underground, we make more money.

Yes.

For about three days.

Then:

no replacement tool handles  
no mine supports  
no fuel  
no new housing  
no crates

Congratulations.

Production collapses.

Later, forestry becomes progressively less labor-intensive because you gain:

better saws  
dedicated lumber crews  
sawmills  
carts  
external suppliers

Eventually wood might become cheap enough that importing bulk timber is preferable to spending valuable high-skilled dwarf labor cutting trees.

That is exactly how one economic bottleneck disappears and another replaces it.

## The wage system should affect actual productive capacity

Pay should not just generate a happiness bonus.

A dwarf's wage creates a **private economic capacity**.

Higher pay allows the dwarf to purchase:

better food  
better lodging  
clothing  
entertainment  
comfort  
personal tools  
family goods

Those purchases improve:

rest quality  
nutrition  
morale  
social stability  
status  
long-term retention

Therefore wages feed back into tomorrow's labor.

You can model motivation loosely as:

\[
M =
f(
\text{Wage Satisfaction},
\text{Needs Met},
\text{Rest},
\text{Housing},
\text{Relationships},
\text{Work Conditions}
)
\]

A dwarf doesn't suddenly swing a pickaxe twice as hard because you raised his wage yesterday.

Instead, several days of improved living standards gradually create a healthier, better rested, more invested worker.

Much more believable.

## The worker wealth pool is the birth of the town

This is one of the strongest mechanics you've introduced.

Initially most currency exists in:

\[
\text{Company Treasury}
\]

You pay wages.

Now money moves into:

\[
\text{Worker Wealth Pool}
\]

Early on, there isn't much for workers to spend it on.

That itself becomes visible.

Perhaps the settlement has:

**Private Wealth: 380 crowns**

but:

**Available Consumer Goods: 74 crowns**

So workers are accumulating money without meaningful ways to spend it.

Borrin tells you:

> "They've got coin now. Nothing to buy with it."

That's the moment the **marketplace** becomes necessary.

Not because you unlocked "Marketplace: Level 6."

Because the economic conditions generated demand for one.

Excellent.

## Businesses should emerge from demand

Once enough private wealth exists, you start seeing opportunities:

**Unmet food demand: High**

Possible institution:

Bakery.

You select a dwarf with:

Cooking: 51  
Business: 22  
Initiative: 67  
Savings: 48 crowns

Maybe she becomes the proprietor.

The company contributes:

160 crowns construction capital.

She contributes:

48 crowns private capital.

Bakery opens.

Now grain enters.

Bread comes out.

Workers buy bread using wages.

The bakery earns money.

Owner pays employees.

Company collects commercial tax.

Money completes a circuit:

\[
\text{Company}
\rightarrow
\text{Wages}
\rightarrow
\text{Workers}
\rightarrow
\text{Businesses}
\rightarrow
\text{Taxes}
\rightarrow
\text{Company}
\]

Now we have an actual dwarf economy rather than just a payroll simulation. 🏘️

## But I would make taxation emerge slowly

At first, I would avoid wage taxation altogether.

The colony is ruined, wages are low, and you're desperately trying to create disposable income.

You actually **want money circulating among dwarves**.

So early revenue might come through:

company-owned resource sales  
stall rents  
trade fees  
business licensing

Then later:

commercial tax  
property tax  
import duties  
luxury taxes

And only once the settlement is wealthy enough:

income tax.

This creates an interesting economic progression.

Early Lord Dwarf asks:

> "How do I get money into the workers' hands?"

Midgame Lord Dwarf asks:

> "How do I keep enough money circulating?"

Late Lord Dwarf asks:

> "How much can I tax without strangling the economy?"

That is a much richer trajectory than just increasing a tax slider.

## Private wealth should not simply be company wealth in disguise

This distinction has to stay meaningful.

Once you pay a dwarf, those coins are **his**.

The company cannot simply reclaim them.

That creates a genuine second economic actor.

A prosperous miner might eventually have:

Savings: 1,200 crowns  
House: privately owned  
Furniture: 260 crowns  
Shares in tavern: 15%  
Family: spouse + 2 children

At that point he's no longer merely "Miner #7."

He's a citizen with capital.

And this creates an entire social transformation.

## Families create the longest-term labor economy

Relationships should slowly introduce:

courtship  
partnership  
marriage

children

But children should be a **very long-term investment**, not free population growth.

Families need:

larger housing  
food

education  
medical care

Children initially subtract labor from the economy.

Eventually:

children → students → apprentices → workers

Then the wild thing happens:

your second generation enters the workforce.

A young dwarf might have:

Father: Master Miner

That dwarf possesses a developmental environment your founding twelve could never have imagined.

Now civilization is literally reproducing competence.

## This also solves workforce replacement

The game starts with:

"Import labor because we need hands."

Then becomes:

"Train the labor we already have."

Then:

"Build institutions that produce skilled labor."

Then:

"Our town now produces its own next generation."

That progression is magnificent because population itself becomes an infrastructure system.

## The main economic eras are becoming very clear

You effectively have five stages:

| Era | Primary constraint | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Salvage Economy** | Raw labor | Everyone does everything |
| **Extraction Economy** | Workforce energy | Mining dominates |
| **Industrial Economy** | Processing + technology | Machines and specialists |
| **Town Economy** | Capital circulation | Businesses and households |
| **Dwarf Civilization** | Institutions + knowledge | Economy becomes partly autonomous |

And there's an important transition hidden inside this.

At first:

**Lord Dwarf allocates dwarves.**

Eventually:

**Lord Dwarf allocates capital.**

That is probably the most important shift in the entire game.

Early game decision:

> "Should Dwarf #6 chop trees tomorrow?"

Late game decision:

> "Should we spend 18,000 crowns expanding the steelworks or subsidizing residential construction?"

Same game.

Completely different scale.

## And one balancing principle will keep everything working

Every advancement should remove an old bottleneck while creating a new one.

Better tools eliminate labor shortage.

But increase metal demand.

Better machinery reduces labor demand.

But creates maintenance demand.

Higher wages improve workers.

But increase consumer expectations.

More private wealth generates businesses.

But creates infrastructure pressure.

Population growth creates workers.

But creates housing and food demand.

Academies create experts.

But remove workers from productive labor while they're training.

Automation creates enormous productivity.

But makes you dependent on engineers and spare parts.

So there is never a final point where the player says:

> "Economy solved."

Instead:

> "I solved the problem we used to have."

And somewhere, Borrin is already holding another ledger. 📒
