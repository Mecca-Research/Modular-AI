Foreign Policy: Confrontation Over Diplomacy

Trump entered office promising to upend conventional U.S. foreign policy, and in eight months he delivered – though not with the cohesive strategic outcomes he touted. Instead, the period was characterized by aborted peace initiatives, escalating conflicts, and erratic shifts in stance that unsettled allies and adversaries alike.

The United States’ standing as a consistent global leader unquestionably declined in this period: allies learned they might need to go it alone or hedge bets, while rivals tested the limits of Trump’s unpredictability.

If there was one arena Trump officials pointed to as positive, it was the campaign against ISIS in Iraq and Syria – which by late 2017 was nearing victory as ISIS’s territorial “caliphate” collapsed under U.S.-led coalition and local forces. Meanwhile, new fires were ignited elsewhere, as detailed above.

Looking ahead, the implications of Trump’s foreign policy approach were worrisome to many analysts. The world order appeared less stable: arms control treaties were at risk, nuclear proliferation pressures were rising (e.g. Saudi Arabia mulled pursuing nuclear technology as Iran and North Korea challenges grew), and conflicts in the Middle East risked drawing in great powers. America’s diplomatic corps was depleted – by mid-2025, dozens of ambassadorships remained vacant or filled by untested loyalists, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio struggled to manage crises on multiple fronts. The future trajectory seemed to hinge on whether Trump’s bold gambits would somehow force adversaries to capitulate (a risky bet), or whether miscalculations and lack of strategic consistency would lead to more wars, more nuclear arms races, and a diminished U.S. role in shaping global norms. Observers noted that America’s adversaries – from Beijing to Moscow – might actually welcome a U.S. that was erratic and internally divided, as it created vacuums for them to fill on trade, technology, and diplomacy.

Economic Policy and the “Big Beautiful Bill”

He also announced a review of Obama-era fuel efficiency standards, a move long sought by Detroit. And his rollback of electric vehicle incentives (the OBBBA phased out EV tax credits from the 2022 climate law

. The United Auto Workers, which had tenuously backed Trump’s tariffs initially, grew anxious that his policies were inconsistent – protective in some ways but harmful in others. By August 2025, with an auto workers’ strike looming (over EV production and wages), Trump’s relationship with the auto sector was mixed: he was hailed for being the first Republican to champion tariffs to protect factories

Energy and Agriculture: Trump unapologetically championed fossil fuels. He repealed Obama’s Clean Power Plan, exited Paris, opened Arctic and offshore areas for drilling, and proclaimed “energy dominance” as a goal. The OBBBA cemented this by removing incentives for solar, wind, and electric vehicles, and by providing unspecified tax breaks for fossil fuel production

The administration’s efforts to subsidize coal (through EPA rule changes) met legal challenges. In agriculture, Trump’s trade conflicts had immediate downsides: China’s retaliation tariffs on U.S. soybeans and pork in 2018 (during the first trade war) devastated many farmers, who only survived via federal bailout payments. In 2025, when Trump re-escalated tariffs, China again targeted U.S. farm goods, and additionally, Trump’s clampdown on immigration contributed to farm labor shortages (as migrant labor became scarcer). Farm state senators from Trump’s party quietly grumbled that the “easy to win” trade war was anything but. Still, Trump remained popular with many farmers due to cultural affinity and his promises to fight for them. He tried to blunt criticisms with moves like year-round E15 ethanol approval to please corn growers, and in the budget bill, he included a provision for regional “economic zones” with reduced tariffs to help agricultural exports

The global climate of trade uncertainty stoked by Trump left many agribusinesses unable to plan ahead, arguably weakening America’s reputation as a reliable supplier.

Technology and Infrastructure: While Trump spoke often about infrastructure (recall his 2017 promise of a “trillion-dollar infrastructure plan”), no large-scale infrastructure bill passed in the first 8 months. Infrastructure-wise, Trump did repackage some existing programs: a new American Infrastructure Privatization initiative was floated in July, encouraging public-private partnerships to fund roads and bridges, but critics noted it was essentially a recycled proposal that had failed to gain traction in 2018. So as of month eight, a grand infrastructure rebuild had not materialized, aside from rhetoric.

Healthcare and Social Programs: Trump had campaigned on repealing Obamacare; an attempt to do so via legislation had famously failed by one vote in 2017. In 2025, rather than another frontal assault on the ACA, Trump used executive tools and the budget to chip away at it. The OBBBA notably did not include a full repeal of the ACA, but it zeroed out remaining Obamacare taxes (like the health insurance tax) and slashed Medicaid, which was expanded under ACA
en.wikipedia.org
. It also imposed the aforementioned work requirements on Medicaid and SNAP. By late 2025, states were seeking waivers to implement those, and projections indicated hundreds of thousands could lose benefits as a result. Additionally, Trump directed HHS (now led by RFK Jr.) to widen short-term “junk” insurance plans and to not enforce the ACA’s individual mandate (which had no penalty after 2019 anyway). These moves, while subtle, contributed to an uptick in uninsured rates and higher premiums for comprehensive plans, according to insurers. Perhaps the most consequential health policy change was RFK Jr.’s overhaul of vaccine policy – a subject detailed in the next section on key figures, but suffice to say it created turmoil in public health agencies and worried medical experts that resurgent diseases or new pandemics might find the U.S. less prepared.

In chronological order, the economic agenda progressed as follows: Winter 2025 – Trump set the trade protectionist tone (TPP exit, NAFTA renegotiation notice, travel ban disrupting tourism somewhat); Spring 2025 – unveiled sweeping budget cuts (March), began tariff actions (April lumber tariff), lobbied behind the scenes for the big tax bill while rallying Rust Belt crowds with tough trade talk; Early Summer – passed the tax & budget bill through the House in May, the Senate by July 1 (with VP JD Vance casting a tiebreaking vote)
en.wikipedia.org
, and signed it July 4. Mid-Late Summer – implemented the bill’s immediate provisions (tax rate extensions took effect, agencies started cutting budgets), escalated the China trade probe (August), and attempted to mitigate political fallout (Trump traveled to farm states in August to announce a new farm aid package, as a band-aid for tariff pain). By September, as the fiscal year 2026 budget planning started, America’s fiscal trajectory was stark: deficits were ballooning, likely exceeding $1.5 trillion annually, even as the administration insisted economic growth (they predicted 4%+ GDP growth) would pay for the cuts – a claim widely doubted by independent economists.

The implications of Trump’s economic program are far-reaching. In the short term, wealthier Americans and corporations benefited significantly from tax relief and deregulation, likely boosting investment and stock buybacks (Wall Street hit record highs through mid-2025). Unemployment remained low, though wage gains were tepid for lower earners. Inequality was set to widen: critics noted the bill lavished permanent tax cuts on heirs and investors while letting a payroll tax cut for low-wage workers expire in 2028, and simultaneously gutting programs that support the poor
en.wikipedia.org
en.wikipedia.org
. In the long term, the combination of higher debt, reduced public investment in education/science, and an eroding social safety net could weaken America’s economic foundation. The CBO warned that the debt-to-GDP ratio, already high, would accelerate upward, potentially raising interest rates and crowding out private investment
en.wikipedia.org
. Meanwhile, states bracing for reduced federal Medicaid and SNAP funds could face budget crunches, potentially leading to service cuts or higher state taxes. The administration, however, touted what it called the “Blue-Collar Boom 2.0” – pointing to modest upticks in manufacturing jobs (some due to defense spending and reshoring from tariffs) and a slight rise in labor force participation as work requirements pushed some into low-paying jobs. Trump himself remained upbeat, declaring in rallies that “our economy is the strongest it’s ever been – we’ve cut your taxes, we’ve ended the war on American energy, and we’re putting America back to work!”. Yet many outside observers believed the apparent sugar-high masked serious structural risks. Summing up: Trump’s early economic performance was a paradox – robust on the surface, but potentially sowing seeds of decline through higher inequality, fiscal strain, and international isolation in trade. As one Democratic senator quipped, “It’s easy to have a short-term boom when you’re borrowing hundreds of billions from the future. The hangover will come – and it won’t be Trump’s rich friends who pay the price.”

Marco Rubio, the Secretary of State, is a more conventional figure in Trump’s cabinet – a sitting Republican senator and onetime presidential rival who aligned with Trump’s agenda. As the nation’s top diplomat, Rubio has been tasked with executing Trump’s unpredictable foreign policy and repairing relations when possible. A hawk on many issues, Rubio has pursued Trump’s line on China and Iran vigorously, while trying to reassure allies privately that the U.S. hasn’t completely abandoned leadership.

You’re describing a phenomenon in very vivid terms, and interestingly, there is a kernel of technical truth behind your metaphor even though it’s not expressed in the standard vocabulary used by ML researchers.

Here’s how what you’re calling “filaments” and “braids” relates to what actually happens in a transformer:

1️⃣ The Actual Flow of Data in a Transformer
Input Representation (large matrix): When you send in a sequence, the embedding layer produces a big matrix: sequence length × embedding dimension. This is your “large token matrix.”
Layer-by-layer Transformation: Each layer applies multi-head self-attention and feed-forward blocks. This keeps the same matrix shape but changes its contents; there isn’t a literal “shrinking pyramid,” but later layers compress and rotate the representation space, often causing features to become more linearly separable (which might feel like narrowing).
Final Projection (probability distribution): The last layer projects the hidden state of each position onto the vocabulary logits to produce a next-token probability vector. That’s your “small token stream.”

So mathematically, it’s not a funnel that literally reduces dimensionality. But conceptually, the effective information becomes more focused and aligned with output tokens as you move upward.

2️⃣ What “Filament Formation” Could Correspond To
Each attention head is like a soft routing mechanism. Some heads learn to strongly attend to very specific token patterns or positional relationships.
Over layers, the residual stream carries a superposition of features from many heads. Some features become dominant; others fade.
If you plot attention or activation paths across layers, you sometimes see diagonal “flows” of influence — almost like filaments connecting input positions to output positions.
Researchers in mechanistic interpretability have mapped these to “circuits” (Anthropic’s term), which is close to your idea of “filaments” in latent space.

So your description of “filament-like” phenomena in latent space is a good metaphor for how correlated features propagate through layers.

3️⃣ “Braiding” and Harmonization
Multi-head attention literally concatenates the outputs of different heads. Each head extracts different features; concatenating them is like weaving strands into a thicker rope.
Residual connections add previous layer information to the current layer — another form of braiding streams together.
However, there’s no explicit graph or topology of filaments; it’s all high-dimensional tensor operations. The “braid” exists in the math of combined vectors, not as a physical lattice.

So your pyramid → filament → braid metaphor is a conceptual map of:

Wide early embeddings (many mixed features).
Narrower, more specialized high-layer features.
Multi-head concatenation (braiding).
