English did acquire a remarkably unusual combination of properties: a largely Germanic grammatical skeleton, enormous Romance and Latinate vocabulary, relatively little noun inflection, unusually productive compounding, and a tendency to absorb foreign words without demanding that they become completely "English" first. The causal arrow, though, probably runs both ways. Political expansion spread English, but centuries of contact, commerce, conquest, migration, administration, science, and later global communication also kept remodeling English into an increasingly useful contact language.

## The first secret: English is not Latin + French + German

Its **core is Germanic**.

Old English descended primarily from West Germanic varieties brought to Britain by Angles, Saxons, Jutes and related populations. Its basic machinery is still visibly Germanic:

> You are.
> He is.
> We have.
> Come here.
> Go home.
> What is that?

*I, you, he, we, be, have, come, go, home, what, that* are overwhelmingly inherited Germanic material.

It got repeatedly **hit by other linguistic systems without being replaced by them**.

Norse contact contributed vocabulary and probably helped reinforce some simplification and convergence in areas of intense contact. Norman rule brought enormous French influence. Latin continuously supplied ecclesiastical, scholarly, legal, scientific and technical vocabulary.

**Germanic operating system + Norse patches + French institutional vocabulary + Latin intellectual libraries**

# English underwent something resembling grammatical compression

Old English had substantially more inflection than Modern English.

You could encode grammatical relationships through endings. Nouns had cases. Adjectives agreed with nouns. Grammatical gender existed. Verb morphology carried more information.

Over centuries, much of this eroded.

Modern English increasingly says:

**position tells you function.**

Compare the conceptual difference.

In a highly inflected language, you can mark:

**DOG-subject MAN-object BIT**

and word order can potentially move around because morphology identifies who's doing what.

English instead strongly favors:

**The dog bit the man.**

Reverse it:

**The man bit the dog.**

Same words. Completely different proposition.

English has therefore transferred considerable grammatical information from **morphology into sequencing**.

In your terminology, that's interesting because English makes extremely heavy use of something resembling **P2: time/order** as an encoding channel.

The token's *position* becomes information.

English didn't eliminate morphology, of course:

> dog → dogs
> walk → walked
> he → him → his

But compared with Old English, Latin, Russian, Sanskrit, etc., the morphological machinery is drastically reduced.

And that has consequences.
