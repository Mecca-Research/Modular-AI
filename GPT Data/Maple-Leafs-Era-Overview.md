The 2021-22 season became a pivotal “make or break” year for the Dubas/Shanahan project. To their credit, the Leafs responded to the prior year’s embarrassment with the best regular season in franchise history. They racked up 115 points (a new club record) and went 54-21-7, finishing second in the Atlantic (only behind the record-setting Florida Panthers). Auston Matthews elevated his game to MVP level: he scored 60 goals, breaking the franchise single-season record and becoming the first NHL player to net 60 in a decade. He earned the Hart Trophy as NHL MVP and a second Rocket Richard Trophy. Marner had a career-best 97 points and a 35-game point streak. The team set all sorts of records offensively and seemed more mature and determined. Even with these achievements, everyone understood that playoff success was the only real measure. The Leafs drew a very difficult first-round matchup: the two-time defending Stanley Cup champion Tampa Bay Lightning. In a back-and-forth series widely regarded as the most entertaining of the 2022 first round, Toronto once again pushed it to seven games. They had Tampa on the ropes – holding a 3-2 series lead after a gutsy Game 5 win – but could not land the knockout punch. Tampa won Game 6 in overtime (Toronto was inches away from winning that one), and then Game 7 in Toronto was a tight defensive battle that ended 2-1 in Tampa’s favor. For the Leafs, it was another first-round exit, their fifth straight in winner-take-all fashion. The chorus of disappointment swelled further, but this time there was also a measure of respect from observers: the Leafs had gone toe-to-toe with the NHL’s best and barely fell short. Toronto’s Core (Matthews, Marner, etc.) actually played well in this series, and it felt less like a choke and more like a near miss against an elite opponent. Still, the bottom line remained – another year, another early exit, making it six consecutive seasons without advancing.

At this juncture, fan frustration was palpable. The Leafs had now proven themselves as excellent in the regular season (five straight 100-point full seasons by 2022

) but seemingly snake-bitten in the playoffs. Voices in media and the fanbase grew louder that something fundamental might need change – “if they couldn’t win now, when would they?” Many pointed out that by running back the same core year after year, the Leafs were bordering on insanity expecting a different result. As Sportsnet later summarized, the primary misstep of the Shanahan era was an “unyielding belief in the Core Four” and a “hesitance to make significant changes” even as first-round exits piled up
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
. Through 2022, Shanahan stuck to his guns, keeping GM Kyle Dubas in charge and re-signing coach Sheldon Keefe to an extension. That continuity move – retaining Keefe after the 2022 loss – was questioned by some, but Shanahan showed faith in his group. Internally, there was a sense that if the process was sound, eventually results would follow. Externally, patience was wearing extremely thin. The Leafs were now carrying the burden of the NHL’s longest Stanley Cup drought (which by 2022 had stretched to 55 years and counting) and had not won a single playoff series since 2004

Breaking the Curse: Finally Tasting Playoff Success (2022–2023)

The spring of 2023 at last brought a breakthrough – albeit one that was both joyful and short-lived. Determined to shake the narrative, GM Kyle Dubas made a flurry of moves ahead of the 2023 trade deadline to address perceived weaknesses. He acquired veterans with championship pedigree and grit: Ryan O’Reilly (2019 Conn Smythe winner) and Noel Acciari from St. Louis, defenseman Luke Schenn (a former Leaf known for physical play), and others like Jake McCabe and Sam Lafferty. These additions signaled an all-in push to support the core with the kind of players who could win puck battles and provide leadership in tough moments. The team finished the 2022-23 regular season with 111 points, again second in the Atlantic (behind the Boston Bruins, who set an NHL record for wins). Their reward was a familiar opponent in the first round: the Tampa Bay Lightning for the second straight year. This time, Toronto would not be denied.

The 2023 first-round series vs Tampa became the moment the fanbase had been craving. In a dramatic six-game series, the Maple Leafs exorcised their first-round demons and defeated the Lightning 4 games to 2, capturing their first playoff series win in 19 years. It wasn’t easy – three of Toronto’s wins came in overtime thrillers on the road in Tampa. The Final Chapter of the Shanahan Era (2023–2025)

The 2023-24 season would prove to be the last under Brendan Shanahan’s leadership – though that wasn’t obvious until the end. Over the summer of 2023, new GM Brad Treliving made a series of roster changes with Shanahan’s blessing. Legacy and Future Outlook

As the Leafs turn the page on the Shanahan era, the franchise finds itself at a crossroads. The legacy of these 11 years is complex. On one hand, Shanahan’s tenure brought the Maple Leafs out of the darkest period of their modern history. The team went from being a league laughingstock to a consistent contender (at least in the regular season), stocked with star talent that he drafted or acquired. Fan engagement and expectations soared during this era – every year the Leafs are touted among the NHL’s elite now, a stark change from the apathy of the early 2010s. Shanahan guided the club to that point: “Shanahan’s guided the club to a place where lofty expectations are warranted and realistic. Before his arrival, that wasn’t the case,” noted Sportsnet
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
. In essence, he raised the floor of the franchise tremendously.

Yet, the ceiling – a Stanley Cup – remained out of reach, and ultimately that is how many will judge the era. The enduring image is not Matthews scoring 60 or winning MVPs, but rather the Leafs shaking hands as losers in yet another Game 7. “There is nothing more I wanted to deliver to our fans,” Shanahan wrote in his farewell, apologizing that he “could not finish the job” of ending the Cup drought
sportsnet.ca
. Indeed, the drought now stands at 58 years (and counting), the longest in NHL history
en.wikipedia.org
en.wikipedia.org
. The fact that archrivals like Montreal and Boston were often the ones to dash Toronto’s hopes (not to mention underdog Montreal in 2021) only added salt to the wounds. Fairly or not, Shanahan’s era will be tagged as one of “tumult, progress and plateauing”
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
 – the team progressed to respectability but plateaued short of true greatness.

Looking ahead, the Maple Leafs face significant questions. With Shanahan gone, MLSE will likely install new leadership (or empower GM Brad Treliving further) to set a fresh vision. One of the biggest immediate issues is Mitch Marner’s future. Marner’s contract expires in 2025, and as Sportsnet highlighted, the timing of Shanahan’s departure and Marner’s no-move clause kicking in left Toronto in a tough spot – there is a real possibility Marner could walk as a free agent if not extended, meaning the Leafs might lose a 100-point, Selke-nominee asset for nothing
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
. This looming decision will be a litmus test for the post-Shanahan regime: do they recommit to Marner (and by extension keep the Core Four model alive), or do they finally break up the core for a different roster construction? Similarly, John Tavares will be at the end of his contract in 2025; at 34, his role and cost will need re-evaluation (will he take a pay cut to stay, or will the captaincy change and age lead to parting ways?). William Nylander’s new extension kicks in, and Auston Matthews remains in his prime under contract. So, the Leafs are assured to still have star talent – but how they complement it or reconfigure it will be crucial.

There is also the matter of team identity. Under Shanahan (and Dubas), the Leafs were built on speed and skill, and only later tried to sprinkle in toughness. The new management might seek a different balance. The brief success of adding players like O’Reilly and Schenn in 2023 indicated that a bit more “sandpaper” can go a long way in the playoffs. Already in 2024-25, Treliving’s influence was seen in adding gritty, defensive-minded players (e.g., Chris Tanev’s presence and leading plus-minus
en.wikipedia.org
). The next few years could see Toronto pivot to a more playoff-geared style, potentially taking a page from teams like the Florida Panthers who ousted them with relentless forechecking and physical play.

One should also note the goaltending and depth moving forward. The Shanahan era never did solidify the goaltender position long-term – Andersen was steady but not a playoff savior, Campbell shone briefly but wasn’t extended, and recent stopgaps have been hit or miss. Finding a true elite goalie or at least a clutch playoff performer in net is high on the wish list. Depth scoring and defense beyond the core will also need continuous renewal, hopefully through better drafting. One critique of the era was the failure to develop many impact players outside the top picks; aside from a few (Hyman, a later-round trade/college signing success, and Morgan Rielly from 2012, or the recent emergence of Matthew Knies, a 2nd-round gem in 2021
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
), the pipeline didn’t yield a lot of difference-makers. Knies in particular stands out as a positive development – the power forward impressed in late 2023 and 2024 and looks to be part of the new core moving forward
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
. His growth may influence how the team handles other wingers.

From a fan perspective, the end of the Shanahan era is met with mixed emotions. There’s gratitude for how he restored pride to the franchise – the memories of Matthews’ four-goal debut, the wild Game 7s (even in defeat they were memorable), Tavares’ signing, and finally slaying the first-round dragon in 2023. But there’s also a sense of “unfinished business”. Leafs fans are desperate for a Stanley Cup, and each year of disappointment adds to a generational burden. By 2025, some of the shine of the rebuild has worn off and pragmatism (if not cynicism) has set in. The core stars are no longer wide-eyed kids; they are mid-20s veterans now. The championship window is still open, but not forever – Matthews and Marner are around 27 as of 2025, theoretically entering their peak years. The philosophy of the next front office might shift to a “win at all costs now” mode, given the maturity of the core, as opposed to the patient build of the Shanahan plan. Indeed, Shanahan’s dismissal itself indicates MLSE’s urgency to “add a new chapter to the Leafs’ championship history” and not settle for being merely good
sportsnet.ca
sportsnet.ca
. Fans will likely have less patience for any step-back or talk of long-term resets – they want immediate playoff success.

In closing, the Brendan Shanahan era will be remembered as one that revitalized Toronto hockey but could not deliver its ultimate promise. It gave the Leafs a foundation of stars and a new identity as a fast, skilled team, and it ended the franchise’s long playoff-series win drought (a modest but symbolic achievement in 2023). However, it fell short of the lofty goal Shanahan set when he took the job: ending the Stanley Cup drought. The next era, under new leadership, will build on the successes and failures of the last 11 years. With a talented core still in place, the challenge is to learn from those failures – perhaps by being more flexible and bolder in altering the mix – in order to achieve what Shanahan’s group could not. The fans, who have endured so much heartbreak, remain hopeful yet anxious. As jerseys thrown on the ice in 2025 showed, their passion can quickly turn to frustration when expectations aren’t met
en.wikipedia.org
. The Shanahan era raised those expectations to a high level; it’s now up to the post-Shanahan Maple Leafs to fulfill them and, finally, reward one of hockey’s most loyal fanbases with a championship chapter to celebrate.
