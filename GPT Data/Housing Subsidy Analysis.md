## Message 2 — Assistant

<!-- Message ID: fb2032ea-b376-4e87-be35-3bfdc7921033; Turn index: 0 -->

Yes. There is a powerful idea inside what you're proposing, but I would change **what the government promises to protect**.

I would not build it as a *Housing Revaluation Subsidy* where Ottawa guarantees homeowners their previous market value. I would build it as a **Housing Balance-Sheet Reset**:

> **Let housing prices fall toward affordability, while preventing the fall from turning into mass negative equity, mortgage defaults, forced sales, and banking losses.**

That distinction turns the idea from something almost fiscally impossible into something that could conceivably be engineered.

### The fundamental problem: value and debt are different things

Suppose a house is selling for $1 million today but a sustainable affordability model says something closer to $500,000. Canada cannot simply announce, "This house is now worth $500,000." Market value is generated by buyers, sellers, credit availability, supply, land scarcity, expected appreciation and other factors.

A better affordability benchmark would also be **local**, rather than based primarily on international house prices. CMHC itself models affordability partly through housing costs relative to local household income, targeting roughly 30% where feasible and, in exceptionally expensive markets, a return toward earlier affordability levels. [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/housing-markets-data-and-research/housing-research/research-reports/accelerate-supply/canadas-housing-supply-shortages-a-new-framework)

But government absolutely **can alter the financial contracts sitting underneath the house**.

And that is where your concept gets interesting.

Consider two owners.

| Owner | Situation | What happens if house falls $1M → $500k? |
|---|---|---|
| A | Bought decades ago for $250k, mortgage paid off | Loses an unrealized paper gain |
| B | Bought recently for $1M with an $800k mortgage | Owes $800k against a $500k asset |

These aren't remotely the same problem.

Giving Owner A $500,000 because their house stopped being worth $1 million would essentially guarantee speculative appreciation using taxpayer money.

Owner B, however, faces a genuine **balance-sheet crisis**. They're $300,000 underwater. They may be unable to refinance, move, sell, or absorb an income shock.

The Bank of Canada explicitly identifies this mechanism: lower house prices themselves don't necessarily hurt a household capable of servicing its mortgage, but falling equity can make refinancing difficult and increase default risk. As of its 2026 Financial Stability Report, the Bank estimates Canada's typical home price was already around 20% below its 2022 peak. [Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-report-2026/households/)

So I think your program should protect **debt solvency rather than peak valuation**.

## Why the full compensation version becomes almost impossible

The numbers become monstrous very quickly.

Canadian household residential real estate was worth about **C$8.47 trillion in Q1 2026**. [Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260612/dq260612a-eng.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

If government somehow compensated every homeowner for:

| National valuation decline | Approx. compensation |
|---:|---:|
| 20% | **C$1.69 trillion** |
| 30% | **C$2.54 trillion** |
| 50% | **C$4.24 trillion** |
| 70% | **C$5.93 trillion** |

That's the fiscal black hole hiding underneath a universal "nobody loses anything" promise.

But Canada's outstanding residential mortgages are much smaller, although still enormous, having crossed **C$2.4 trillion in December 2025**. [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/media-newsroom/news-releases/2026/renewal-wave-peaks-still-dominates-mortgage-market)

And only a fraction of those mortgages would actually become dangerously underwater under a moderate price correction.

That's the opening.

## Your strongest advantage: it breaks the crash-feedback loop

Imagine prices decline 30%.

Ordinarily:

**prices fall → equity disappears → refinancing becomes harder → distressed sales increase → more houses hit the market → prices fall further → lenders tighten credit → buyers disappear → more forced sales**

That's how an orderly correction can mutate into a financial accident.

Your reset institution could cut that loop.

Instead:

**prices fall → affordability improves → vulnerable mortgages enter restructuring → government/lender absorb part of the legacy debt → homeowner remains housed → forced-sale pressure stays limited**

That is genuinely valuable.

And Canada currently has the sort of highly leveraged household sector where such an institution could matter. The Bank of Canada still describes household debt relative to income as elevated, although current mortgage losses remain broadly contained. [Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/financial-stability-report/financial-stability-report-2026/households/)

## But there's another huge advantage

It separates **the price of housing from the financial system's dependence on high housing prices**.

That's the dragon Canada has trapped itself inside.

If houses remain expensive, younger and lower-wealth households get locked out.

If houses crash rapidly, leveraged owners and parts of the financial system get hurt.

So public policy becomes biased toward perpetually supporting prices.

Your institution effectively says:

> "We will permit the asset price to normalize without requiring every legacy mortgage written during the bubble to normalize instantaneously with it."

That's much more sophisticated than simply subsidizing first-time buyers, because subsidizing buyers often creates **additional purchasing power chasing the same houses**, which can itself support higher prices.

## Here's how I would redesign your $1-million example

Suppose:

**Purchase price:** $1,000,000  
**Original down payment:** $200,000  
**Mortgage:** $800,000  
**New sustainable market value:** $500,000

Instead of Ottawa paying the owner $500,000 in cash, create a **Mortgage Reset Facility**.

The $800,000 mortgage might be transformed into something such as:

**$450,000 ordinary mortgage**

plus

**$150,000 government shared-equity/recovery claim**

with

**$100,000 lender writedown**

and perhaps

**$100,000 government loss absorption**

depending upon the exact program.

I'm deliberately using illustrative numbers rather than prescribing those percentages.

The homeowner now services something much closer to the economic value of the property.

The bank avoids a catastrophic default.

The government hasn't handed someone hundreds of thousands in free cash.

And the government's shared-equity claim could recover money if the property eventually appreciates again.

That last part is crucial.

### Government shouldn't simply eat the loss. It should warehouse the loss.

That's a much better financial architecture.

Create a Crown vehicle, perhaps through or alongside CMHC, capable of acquiring impaired portions of mortgages in exchange for long-duration government-backed securities.

In effect:

**Bank:** "I have $800k mortgage exposure."

**Reset Corporation:** "We'll restructure it. You take a negotiated haircut. We'll take part of the impaired tranche."

**Homeowner:** "My mortgage is now survivable."

**Government:** "If this house appreciates substantially or is eventually sold, part of our contribution is recoverable."

Now you've essentially created a **household-resolution corporation**, analogous to the institutions governments create when banking systems themselves become distressed.

And Canada already possesses an unusually useful institutional skeleton for something like this in CMHC, which operates directly in mortgage insurance, securitization and housing finance. [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/media-newsroom/news-releases/2026/renewal-wave-peaks-still-dominates-mortgage-market)

## Who gets protected becomes the political battlefield

I would make the program extremely asymmetric.

It should favour:

- primary residences;
- recent purchasers caught at elevated valuations;
- households facing genuine negative equity;
- people who can afford a realistically resized mortgage;
- owner equity actually contributed through down payments and principal repayment.

It should **not** fully protect speculative appreciation, investors, luxury properties, second homes or people who repeatedly leveraged accumulated housing gains.

Suppose somebody bought a house in 1998 for $250,000, paid it off, watched it rise to $1.2 million, and it subsequently falls to $700,000.

They have not suffered a $500,000 debt loss.

Giving them $500,000 from taxpayers would be extraordinarily difficult to justify, particularly when some of those taxpayers are renters who were priced out of housing in the first place.

That is probably the biggest equity problem with the original version of your proposal.

## There's also one problem your program cannot solve

**Physical scarcity.**

You can rewrite every mortgage contract in Canada and still have precisely the same number of bedrooms Monday morning.

CMHC estimates that restoring affordability requires roughly **430,000 to 480,000 housing starts annually through 2035**, versus a business-as-usual projection around 245,000 to 250,000. [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/housing-markets-data-and-research/housing-research/research-reports/accelerate-supply/canadas-housing-supply-shortages-a-new-framework)

So the reset has to operate alongside enormous supply reform.

Otherwise something peculiar happens.

Government declares that houses should be affordable at $500,000.

Thousands of households can now afford $500,000.

There aren't enough houses.

They bid against each other.

$510k.

$550k.

$625k.

And presto 🪄, you've regenerated the bubble.

The durable solution therefore has four interacting components:

**price normalization + debt restructuring + huge supply expansion + suppression of speculative leverage.**

Remove one and the others start fighting each other.

## There are historical cousins to your idea

Nobody has really performed the exact national asset-price reset you're describing, but governments have done pieces of it.

During the Great Depression, the American **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** purchased and refinanced distressed mortgages. It eventually refinanced roughly one million homes and substantially altered mortgage terms. [FRASER](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/frbsl_news/2008-05-02-does-the-great-depression-hold-the-answers-for-the-current-mortgage-distress.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Iceland went considerably further after its financial crisis, implementing household mortgage debt relief, including principal reductions. An IMF assessment describes a 2014 program involving an across-the-board reduction of roughly 13% for qualifying inflation-indexed mortgage principal. [elibrary.imf.org](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/2014/226/article-A001-en.xml?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So the conceptual family already exists:

**government absorbs or restructures legacy debt so household balance sheets can recover.**

Your proposal extends that idea into something more ambitious: using debt restructuring deliberately to make a housing-price normalization politically and financially survivable.

## And yes, government is essentially the only actor capable of doing it at national scale

But I would amend your statement slightly:

**Only the state can anchor it, but the state cannot execute it alone.**

Banks cannot collectively perform massive voluntary principal reductions without destroying shareholder capital and potentially destabilizing themselves.

The Bank of Canada could supply liquidity, but turning hundreds of billions of private mortgage losses into public losses is fundamentally a **fiscal policy decision**, not ordinary central-bank monetary policy.

CMHC has mortgage-finance infrastructure but does not independently possess unlimited fiscal capacity. [Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/media-newsroom/news-releases/2026/renewal-wave-peaks-still-dominates-mortgage-market)

And Canada has another complication: property and civil-rights jurisdiction lies heavily with the provinces, while housing responsibilities and programs span federal, provincial, territorial and municipal governments. [Legislative Open Parliament](https://lop.parl.ca/sites/PublicWebsite/default/en_CA/ResearchPublications/201935E?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So the machinery would probably have to look something like:

**Federal Treasury + CMHC + banks + credit unions + provinces + mortgage insurers + municipalities**

with Ottawa providing the enormous sovereign balance sheet at the centre.

## My verdict

I think the **original $1M → $300k and government pays the homeowner $700k formulation does not work**. It socializes gigantic amounts of private paper wealth and could cost trillions.

But the underlying idea contains something genuinely potent.

Change the objective from:

> **Protect every homeowner's old valuation**

to:

> **Protect households and the financial system from insolvency while allowing housing valuations to fall.**

Then you have something worth modelling seriously.

I'd call it the **Canadian Housing Balance-Sheet Reset**, with three guarantees:

**No guarantee of peak property value.  
No guarantee of speculative profit.  
A conditional guarantee against catastrophic negative-equity cascades for ordinary owner-occupied housing.**

That gives Canada a path that isn't simply **"keep the bubble forever"** versus **"blow up the bubble and see who survives."**

It creates a third door. 🏠🔧

And that's probably where the most interesting version of your proposal lives.
