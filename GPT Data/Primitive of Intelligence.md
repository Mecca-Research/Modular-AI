## Your detectability point is right, but it needs one extra gear

You asked: what are the mechanisms of detectability?

Your answer, that it comes from frequency and energy differences, is good, but not yet complete.

Detectability is not just “difference in energy/frequency.”  
It is:

\[
\text{Detectability} = \text{difference} + \text{coupling} + \text{resolution} + \text{persistence relative to noise}
\]

Because a difference can exist and still not be detectable by any relevant subsystem.

So the mechanism is:

### 1. Contrast
Two states differ in some physically consequential property:
- frequency
- phase
- charge
- spin
- position
- concentration
- curvature
- topology
- field intensity

### 2. Coupling
Another system must interact differently with those two states.

\[
s_a \neq s_b \quad \text{only matters if} \quad I(s_a) \neq I(s_b)
\]

### 3. Threshold / resolution
The interaction must exceed noise or ambiguity.

### 4. Temporal availability
The difference must remain available long enough to be sampled.

So yes, frequency-energy differences are part of detectability, but the full primitive is:

\[
P_3 = \text{differentially couplable distinguishability}
\]

That phrase is ugly, but it bites the right bone.
