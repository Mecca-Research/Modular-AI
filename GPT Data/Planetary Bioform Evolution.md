Yes. There is a scientifically interesting version of this idea, but I would change one word: rather than Earth literally **evolving toward** a planetary gelatinous organism, ask whether biological evolution could progressively **capture larger fractions of the planet's fluid, mineral, and atmospheric interfaces into living or biologically regulated matter**.

That version gets surprisingly deep.

### Earth already has the embryonic ingredients

Right now we tend to divide Earth into neat boxes:

$$
\text{rock} \;|\; \text{water} \;|\; \text{air} \;|\; \text{life}
$$

But biology doesn't respect those boundaries very well.

A microbial mat is water + cells + extracellular polymers + minerals. Soil is minerals + water + gases + organisms + organic polymers. Biofilms can be mostly hydrated extracellular matrix rather than cells. Marine snow, planktonic gels, mucus networks, reefs, stromatolites, fungal networks and bacterial colonies all create **intermediate states between organism and environment**.

6

So your gelatinous ocean doesn't actually require water itself to become alive. Something stranger and more plausible can happen:

$$
\text{water}
\rightarrow
\text{water containing organisms}
\rightarrow
\text{organisms embedded in hydrated polymers}
\rightarrow
\text{water-dominated biological matrix}
$$

At some point asking **"Is this water containing life, or life containing water?"** becomes partly a matter of scale.

Humans are already roughly that kind of object. Cells themselves are mostly aqueous chemical reactors enclosed by membranes. A gigantic hydrated biological matrix is therefore not chemically absurd.

### Now give evolution hundreds of millions of years

Suppose some lineage becomes spectacularly successful at producing extracellular polymers.

Initially it makes films around colonies.

Then centimeter-thick mats.

Then meter-scale floating structures.

Then enormous colonial networks extending through nutrient-rich surface waters.

Natural selection doesn't need to "want" a planetary organism. Each incremental step merely has to reproduce better than competing arrangements.

And this is where your **destroy → rebuild → destroy → rebuild** mechanism matters.

The planet becomes an evolutionary laboratory:

$$
B_t
\xrightarrow{\text{selection}}
B_{t+1}
\xrightarrow{\text{climate catastrophe}}
B'
\xrightarrow{\text{selection}}
B''
\xrightarrow{\text{mass extinction}}
B'''
$$

Billions upon billions of generations can explore configurations that seem fantastically improbable from our little observational window.

But there is an important constraint.

## The ocean probably cannot simply turn into one uniform blob

Life needs **gradients**.

Energy differences drive metabolism:

$$
\Delta G < 0
$$

Life exploits differences in light, temperature, acidity, redox potential, salinity and chemical concentration.

A completely homogeneous planetary jelly would actually destroy many of the gradients that sustain it.

So I would predict something much more interesting than a uniform gelatinous Earth:

**a differentiated planetary biomass.**

Imagine an ocean hundreds of millions of years from now containing gigantic vertically organized biological layers.

The sunlit upper layer harvests photons.

Below it are nutrient-processing layers.

Farther down are anaerobic metabolic communities.

Mineral interfaces become digestion zones.

Hydrothermal vents become chemosynthetic organs.

Currents distribute metabolites between regions.

The distinction between "ecosystem" and "superorganism" starts getting fuzzy.

You would have something resembling:

$$
\text{Ocean} + \text{biosphere}
\rightarrow
\text{planetary metabolic architecture}.
$$

Not necessarily one genome. Not necessarily one consciousness. Probably not even one species.

Rather, **millions of mutually dependent lineages forming one vertically integrated chemical system.**

That is much closer to your idea of a *vertically integrated single biomass with diverse forms*.

### And then comes the atmosphere

Here physics pushes back harder.

You asked whether the atmosphere could evolve into something resembling **dense liquid-air biomatter**.

Not under Earth's present pressure and temperature. Nitrogen and oxygen remain gases under ordinary surface conditions. Biology can't negotiate with phase diagrams. 😆

But biology could colonize atmospheric volume far more extensively than it currently does.

Imagine microscopic organisms evolving extraordinarily effective flotation mechanisms: hydrogen-filled structures, methane production, extremely low-density tissues, electrostatic interactions, enormous membrane sails or combinations of them.

Then clouds themselves become ecological habitats.

Water droplets already provide temporary microscopic aqueous environments. An organism capable of nucleating droplets around itself could begin manipulating cloud formation.

That creates a fascinating evolutionary pathway:

$$
\text{airborne microbe}
\rightarrow
\text{cloud colonizer}
\rightarrow
\text{cloud engineer}
\rightarrow
\text{persistent atmospheric colony}.
$$

Instead of **liquid air**, you'd get something more physically plausible:

$$
\text{gas}
+
\text{water droplets}
+
\text{organic polymers}
+
\text{cells}
+
\text{aerosols}.
$$

Essentially **biological fog**.

A sufficiently elaborate version could form transient membranes, filaments, sheets and floating colonies kilometers across while remaining overwhelmingly tenuous compared with oceanic biomass.

And now your planet has three connected biological domains:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Lithic biomass}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Oceanic biomass}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Atmospheric biomass}
}
$$

Material rains downward. Spores move upward. Oceans evaporate water into aerial ecosystems. Atmospheric colonies alter precipitation. Rain carries nutrients and organisms back into terrestrial matrices. Rock-weathering organisms release minerals into the cycle.

That would be a genuinely different kind of Earth.

## But five billion years creates a gigantic problem

The Sun.

Five billion years is approximately the timescale on which the Sun leaves its present main-sequence state and develops toward the red-giant phase. Long before then, increasing solar luminosity is expected to make Earth's surface progressively less hospitable. Earth's oceans are not expected simply to remain sitting here for another five billion years.

So the uninterrupted trajectory

$$
\text{Earth today}
\rightarrow
\text{gelatinous super-Earth in 5 Gyr}
$$

isn't realistic under standard stellar evolution.

But that actually makes your thought experiment more interesting.

Give biology **hundreds of millions rather than five billion years**, and there is still an enormous evolutionary state space available. Or alter the astronomical boundary conditions, such as future orbital migration, engineered solar shielding, radically different atmospheric feedbacks, or a hypothetical analogous planet orbiting a longer-lived star, and then you can legitimately ask what happens when biological evolution gets **multi-billion-year continuity**.

And there is another profound point hiding in your proposal.

### The ultimate trajectory may not be "life covering the planet."

It could be:

**life progressively incorporating the planet.**

Early life:

$$
\text{Life} \ll \text{Environment}
$$

Later biosphere:

$$
\text{Life} \leftrightarrow \text{Environment}
$$

Extreme mature biosphere:

$$
\text{Life} \supset \text{large portions of Environment}
$$

Minerals become structural components.

Ocean water becomes intracellular and extracellular medium.

Atmospheric gases become continuously biologically cycled.

Sediments become biological archives.

Weather becomes partially biologically regulated.

Geochemistry becomes increasingly inseparable from metabolism.

At that point an alien geologist landing there might have enormous difficulty saying where **the organism ends and the planet begins**.

And I think that's the strongest form of your hypothesis.

Not:

> *Earth turns into a giant jellyfish.*

But:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{Abiotic planet}
\rightarrow
\textbf{biosphere}
\rightarrow
\textbf{bio-geochemical planet}
\rightarrow
\textbf{planet-scale metabolic architecture}
}
$$

Earth has already made the first two transitions.

Whether evolution can ever make the third transition so extreme that **ocean, atmosphere, crust and biology become functionally inseparable** is a genuinely fascinating question.

In your P/F language, that would represent something remarkable: **P12 multi-agent ecological coupling gradually producing higher-order attractors and persistent feedback structures across planetary scales.** The "organism" would not initially be an organism at all. It would emerge from coupled ecosystems whose collective feedback increasingly controls the substrate on which those ecosystems depend.

The planet doesn't wake up one morning as a creature.

The boundary just gets blurrier and blurrier until, perhaps, there is no clean boundary left. 🌍🧫🫧

Exactly, with one important correction to the clock: **Earth probably does not have five billion more years of surface habitability**, even though the Sun has roughly that long before its red-giant evolution becomes dominant. Increasing solar luminosity is expected to wreck Earth's present climate much earlier. So there are really two clocks:

\[
T_{\text{natural biosphere}} < T_{\text{Earth}} < T_{\text{Sun}}
\]

And that makes your idea more interesting, because Earth's future can be treated as a **constrained trajectory through configuration space**, rather than simply "what happens in five billion years?"

### Earth as a configuration-search system

Your "not too much of anything" observation can be formalized.

Let the planetary state be

\[
X(t)=
\{T,P,H_2O,CO_2,O_2,N_2,pH,S,E,M,B,\ldots\}
\]

where \(T\) is temperature, \(P\) pressure, \(S\) salinity, \(E\) available energy gradients, \(M\) accessible minerals and \(B\) the biosphere.

Habitability isn't one optimal point. It's a region:

\[
\mathcal H=\{X:C_i(X)\leq0\}
\]

There can be hot ecosystems, cold ecosystems, acidic ecosystems, alkaline ecosystems, wet ecosystems and extraordinarily dry ones. But eventually every dimension hits boundaries beyond which complex chemistry cannot maintain itself.

Earth therefore wanders through a gigantic **habitable state-space**.

And evolution searches *within* that changing state-space.

That distinction is powerful:

\[
\text{stellar evolution}
\rightarrow
\text{planetary boundary conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{geochemical states}
\rightarrow
\text{ecological opportunities}
\rightarrow
\text{biological adaptation}.
\]

Life doesn't control the first term naturally, but it increasingly modifies everything downstream.

### And something unprecedented has now appeared

For most of Earth's history, configuration search was blind.

Mutation happens. Environments change. Selection filters the results.

Something like:

\[
X_t \rightarrow
\text{variation}
\rightarrow
\text{selection}
\rightarrow
X_{t+1}.
\]

Technological intelligence introduces another operator:

\[
X_t
\xrightarrow{\text{model}}
\hat X_{t+n}
\xrightarrow{\text{choose}}
X^*
\xrightarrow{\text{intervene}}
X_{t+1}.
\]

That's qualitatively unusual.

A biosphere has produced something capable of **constructing models of the biosphere's future and intentionally modifying some of the parameters determining that future**.

Not unlimited control, certainly. We can't negotiate with thermodynamics, abolish solar evolution, or simply specify Earth's next geological epoch.

But we can already modify genomes, redirect rivers, synthesize molecules, alter atmospheric composition, move species between continents, engineer ecosystems, manipulate minerals, and increasingly model coupled climate-biological systems.

So biological evolution has acquired a primitive form of **foresight-mediated configuration search**.

That fits beautifully into your F-ladder.

Blind evolutionary adaptation resembles an enormous distributed optimization process. Technological intelligence introduces something closer to **F6 predictive feedback**:

\[
\boxed{
\text{observe}
\rightarrow
\text{model}
\rightarrow
\text{predict}
\rightarrow
\text{intervene}
\rightarrow
\text{observe}
}
\]

If civilization survives long enough, the really profound transition wouldn't necessarily be colonizing another planet.

It would be when life learns to **control the astronomical boundary conditions of its own habitat**.

Move asteroids.

Control atmospheric composition.

Regulate planetary albedo.

Manipulate orbital parameters.

Eventually perhaps migrate Earth outward as solar luminosity increases, or abandon Earth while carrying terrestrial life's evolutionary lineage elsewhere.

Then the old equation changes.

For almost four billion years:

\[
\text{Life adapts to Earth}.
\]

Technological civilization potentially introduces:

\[
\boxed{\text{Life adapts Earth to life}.}
\]

And eventually:

\[
\boxed{\text{Life adapts astronomical systems to life}.}
\]

That's the escape hatch from the closed loop you described.

If nothing accomplishes it, Earth's enormous evolutionary experiment eventually terminates. Oceans disappear, complex surface ecosystems collapse, remaining refugia disappear, and Earth's geological record becomes the fossilized output of a finite experiment.

But if some descendant of terrestrial life learns to cross that boundary, then Earth was never the final organism or destination.

It was the **incubator**.

And terrestrial evolution's most consequential product might not ultimately be humans, animals, plants, or even intelligence itself. It might be the emergence of a system capable of taking the evolutionary process that created it and **carrying that process beyond the physical lifetime of its originating planet and star**.

Then the loop doesn't close.

It branches. 🌍 → ☀️ → ✦✦✦
